# Counterpoint and recurring themes: research register

Checked 25 November 2026. This extends the [earlier repertoire register](repertoire-sources.md) beyond fugues, with particular attention to Bartók. It records existing scholarship and data, **not newly completed analyses or diagrams**. The current implemented collection remains [data/index.json](../data/index.json).

The selection favors identifiable motifs, recurring themes, imitation between parts, and transformations with score locations. General program notes and unverified score videos are not treated as analytical ground truth. Priority and proposed visualizations below are editorial recommendations for Structures.

## Recommended starting points

| Goal | Best candidates | Why start here? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Small Bartók examples | Duo 37; *Mikrokosmos* 141 and 57; first Dialogue | Clearly documented imitation, inversion and changing delays |
| Larger Bartók architecture | *Music for Strings, Percussion and Celesta* I/III/IV; Quartet 5/I | Subject returns, cross-movement transformation and reversed thematic order |
| Instrument-to-instrument exchange | Sonata for Two Pianos and Percussion I; *Cantata profana* I | Concrete passages with identified instrumental or choral entries |
| Beyond melodic subjects | *With Drums and Pipes*; Ligeti, *Automne à Varsovie* | Rhythm and simultaneous tempo layers |
| Existing encoded analysis | CRIM; BPS-Motif; JKU-PDD | Actual analytical observations or occurrence coordinates, with different reuse constraints |

These priorities reflect suitability for this viewer, not a ranking of the scholarship. The strongest Bartók material found is mostly human-readable analysis requiring transcription. No complete, recording-aligned Bartók recurrence corpus was verified in the resources checked.

## How to read the register

- **Encoded annotations:** analytical observations already identify musical positions or notes; conversion and validation are still needed.
- **Detailed analysis:** the full source was inspected and contains useful examples, tables, or measure-specific discussion; entry starts and ends still need score checking and transcription.
- **Supporting source:** useful context, score material, or theme exemplars, without a verified inventory of recurrences.
- **Bibliographic lead:** a relevant reference whose substantive analysis was not inspected.

Open access and permission to reproduce diagrams are separate questions. Rights notes describe the notices found, not a blanket license for quoted scores or recordings. No source in this register supplies an alignment to an arbitrary recording.

## Bartók: compact studies with particularly clear contrapuntal ideas

### Forty-Four Duos no. 37, “Prelude and Canon”

**Detailed analysis; very high priority.** Yusuke Nakahara, [“A Triumph of Musical Order? Multiple Sources of Inspiration in ‘Prelude and Canon,’ Forty-Four Duos no. 37”](https://real.mtak.hu/144787/1/ATriumphofMusicalOrderMultipleSourcesofInspirationinPreludeandCanonForty-FourDuosno.37.pdf), *Studia Musicologica* 62/1–2 (2021), 117–138, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1556/6.2021.00008).

The article connects the folk-tune head motif to the two violin parts, compares successive statements, and examines three canons: the leader remains on E while the following voice starts on G, A, and B, with delays of one, two, and three quarter notes. See the analytical discussion on pp. 123–126, Table 1, and the canon discussion and examples later in the article. It also distinguishes the published piece from draft stages.

**Proposed diagram:** two voice rows, motif identity, entry pitch and imitation delay. This is an unusually clear small-scale teaching example. Transcribe the published version rather than treating draft examples as its final notation. The article explicitly states **CC BY 4.0** on p. 138; underlying musical material needs its own provenance. [BMC recording and track list: Barnabás Kelemen / Katalin Kokas](https://bmcrecords.hu/en/albums/barna-kelemen-katalin-kokas-bartok-sonata-for-solo-violin-44-duos).

### Mikrokosmos no. 141, “Subject and Reflection”; no. 57, “Accents”

**Detailed analysis; very high priority.** Yusuke Nakahara, [*Genesis and the ‘Spirit’ of Bartók’s Mikrokosmos*](https://bartok-osszkiadas.zti.hu/wp-content/uploads/nakahara-disszertacio.pdf), PhD dissertation, Liszt Academy (2020). The 420-page PDF was downloaded and inspected; printed pagination differs from PDF page numbers.

For **no. 141**, pp. 244–249 (PDF pp. 266–270), Examples 8-38–8-43, explain recurrent refrains, mirror motion and increasingly displaced imitation. Refrain locations include mm. 1–14, 23–29, 40–46 and 63ff.; the last introduces changing delays, while mm. 47–62 use eighth-note displacement. **No. 57**, p. 192 n. 26, identifies canons at different delays in mm. 1–6 and 7–12, then inverted material at mm. 21–26 and 35ff. No. 140 is a related variation-study candidate in chapter 8.

**Proposed diagrams:** left/right-hand rows with recurrence and inversion labels; show changing delay as an explicit relationship. These are analyses to transcribe, not downloadable annotation files. No open adaptation license was identified in the dissertation. [Listening candidate for no. 141: Bartók, platform-listed historical performance](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ga5NppEvxk0); recording metadata only, no synchronization verified.

### Nine Little Piano Pieces, first Dialogue

**Detailed analysis; high priority.** László Vikárius, [“Bartók’s Late Adventures with Kontrapunkt”](https://real.mtak.hu/65346/1/smus.47.2006.3-4.14.pdf), *Studia Musicologica* 47/3–4 (2006), 395–416, especially pp. 400–402, Examples 3–4. [Repository record](https://real.mtak.hu/65346/), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1556/SMus.47.2006.3-4.14).

The first Dialogue is discussed as continuous two-part canon whose temporal and pitch distances change between sections. The temporal distance contracts from a breve to a whole note (semibreve) and then a quarter note; Example 4 compares thematic heads at mm. 1–3, 8–10, 16–18 and 29–31. The discussion separates final notation from the original draft.

**Proposed diagram:** a compact comparison of successive canon types and thematic heads. It provides a more direct counterpoint case than a general formal outline. The full institutional PDF was inspected. No open adaptation license was verified. [Performer-hosted listening: Robert von Heeren, Dialogue I](https://robertvonheeren.bandcamp.com/track/dialogue-i-moderato-e-major-sz-82-no-1).

### Mikrokosmos no. 151 and related piano miniatures

**Detailed analysis; medium/high priority.** Fallon S. Carpenter, [*Bartók in the 20th-century Theory Classroom: A Structural Analysis of Select Pieces from Mikrokosmos Volumes 5 and 6*](https://cardinalscholar.bsu.edu/items/97483666-d7d1-4580-9bad-9bf12e8dbc2b), Ball State University, M.M. thesis (2018). [Full PDF](https://cardinalscholar.bsu.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/fabe0db7-5d66-4d67-a3b5-8375d6f4f8b3/content) downloaded and inspected.

For **no. 151**, pp. 51–54 and Example 4.9 identify rhythmic material switching between hands at mm. 9, 17, 33, 39, 43, 44, 45 and 50. For **no. 140**, pp. 21–24, Examples 2.8–2.11, relate theme, variations and interludes. The thesis also covers nos. 125, 130, 133, 135, 142 and 144.

**Proposed diagram:** rhythmic-pattern identity by hand, with formal sections. Its score-specific observations are useful; a rhythmic pattern should not automatically be labeled a complete melodic subject. Broader numerical interpretations are separate from the entry evidence. No annotation download or open adaptation license was verified.

### Out of Doors I, “With Drums and Pipes”

**Detailed analysis; high priority for rhythmic counterpoint.** John Roeder, [“Pulse Streams and Problems of Grouping and Metrical Dissonance in Bartók’s ‘With Drums and Pipes’”](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.01.7.1/mto.01.7.1.roeder.html), *Music Theory Online* 7/1 (2001).

Sections 2 and 5–8 examine the two parts in **mm. 20–69**, with annotated figures and measure-specific accounts of motivic blocks and interacting pulse streams. The article includes audiovisual examples with a scrolling annotated score; their legacy QuickTime playback was not tested. This is particularly useful when the recurring material is rhythmic or chordal rather than a singable fugue subject.

**Proposed diagram:** two part rows plus optional pulse layers, distinguishing repeated material from the analyst’s inferred pulses. The article’s figures are detailed source material, not a verified annotation export. MTO reserves authors’ republication rights; no open adaptation license was verified.

### Mikrokosmos no. 131, “Fourths”

**Detailed analysis; exploratory priority.** Scott Murphy, [“Considering Network Recursion and Bartók’s ‘Fourths’”](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.07.13.3/mto.07.13.3.murphy.html), *Music Theory Online* 13/3 (2007), especially part II, paragraphs 9 onward and Figures 1–2.

The opening four bars are examined through mirrored right/left-hand motion, deviations from symmetry and relationships between local gestures and larger patterns. This is a good source for a transformation/network layer alongside a timeline, rather than a straightforward list of subject entries. The article explicitly discusses competing analytical readings and the limits of the method’s applicability. No machine-readable annotation export was verified; MTO reserves authors’ republication rights; no open adaptation license was verified.

## Bartók: chamber works and larger formal designs

### String Quartet no. 5, first movement

**Detailed analysis; very high priority beyond fugue.** László Somfai, [“Bartók’s Most Daring Concept of a Palindromic Structure”](https://real.mtak.hu/144810/1/BartoksMostDaringConceptofaPalindromicStructureTheSonata-FormOpeningMovementoftheFifthQuartet.pdf), *Studia Musicologica* 62/3–4 (2021), 197–211, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1556/6.2021.00015).

Plate 1, p. 202, translates Bartók’s own formal outline; pp. 200–201 reproduce the originals. Themes return inverted and in reversed order. Analytical anchors include D–F development, F–G inverted first theme, G–J reversed recapitulation and J–end coda. The composer also allows alternative boundary interpretations.

**Proposed diagram:** thematic families across the sonata design, with inversion links. Reversed theme order is not literal note-by-note retrograde. Convert rehearsal letters into measures and trace individual instruments before adding entry rows. The article is **CC BY 4.0**; preserve attribution and separately identify historical/third-party material.

### Sonata for Two Pianos and Percussion, first movement

**Detailed analysis; high priority.** Emöke Ujj-Hilliard, [*An Analysis of the Genesis of Motive, Rhythm, and Pitch…*](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc4480/), UNT DMA (2004), [full PDF](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc4480/m2/1/high_res_d/dissertation.pdf).

Pages 67–70 map the movement through m. 443: fugato at 133–160, Piano II’s ostinato against Piano I at 195–207, exchanged roles at 232–261, and returning fugato from 332. Page 87 locates successive subject beginnings at 332/339/346/353. Its phrase “after eight measures” conflicts with those seven-measure differences; check the score.

**Proposed diagram:** two piano rows with motif/ostinato identities and exchanged roles, supplemented by score-verified percussion. Keep formal sections separate from individual subject statements. Full institutional PDF inspected; no open adaptation license or encoded entry dataset established.

### Sonata for Solo Violin, second movement, Fuga

**Detailed analysis; high priority.** Oliver Yatsugafu, [*Performance-Practice Issues in Bartók’s Sonata for Solo Violin (1944)*](https://openscholar.uga.edu/record/7873), University of Georgia DMA (2011), [PDF](https://openscholar.uga.edu/record/7873/files/yatsugafu_oliver_201108_dma.pdf).

Pages 46–48 describe the 107-measure movement. Examples 20–23 show four exposition statements at mm. 1–5, 6–11, 11–15 and 16–20. Later discussion covers inversion at 44–50 and ordinary/inverted motives differentiated by arco/pizzicato at 63–76.

**Proposed diagram:** four implied contrapuntal voices within one instrument. These are not four performing instruments. The source uses “counter-subject” for an answer; normalize terminology and check overlaps/endpoints against the score. Full PDF inspected; its score-reproduction permission is not a general license to reuse those engravings.

### Contrasts, first movement

**Detailed textual analysis; figures still to obtain.** Daphne Leong, with Daniel Silver and Jennifer John, [“Rhythm in the First Movement of Bartók’s Contrasts: Performance and Analysis”](https://voljournals.utk.edu/gamut/article/pubid/vol1-iss1-3/), *Gamut* 1/1 (2008), article 3. [Original-article mirror](https://www.academia.edu/169349152/Rhythm_in_the_First_Movement_of_Bart%C3%B3k_s_Contrasts_Performance_and_Analysis).

Pages 23–25 identify three-instrument imitation at 57–71, then piano dominance at 72–84; imitation distances contract while subsequent piano chains expand. Earlier examples connect thematic identities and instrumental alternation.

**Proposed diagram:** clarinet, violin and piano, with rhythmic transformation labels. The original article text was read through a mirror; the migrated official site blocked automated access and its figures were not obtained. Obtain legible figures before transcription. Copyright retained by Newfound Press; no encoded annotations or timing map verified.

### String Quartet no. 3 and rhythmic cells across the quartets

**Detailed analyses; selected passages.** Deborah Mawer, [“Exploring Complementation in Bartók’s Third Quartet”](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.07.13.4/mto.07.13.4.mawer.html), *MTO* 13/4 (2007). Paragraphs 13 and 24–26, Examples 1–6, examine the opening, imitation at mm. 6–20 and a new division of pitch material from 21. Useful for relationships between complementary parts, rather than labeling every pitch collection a subject. Official text and examples inspected; MTO copyright and separate score permissions apply.

Cheryl D. Bocanegra, [*Accent and Grouping Structures in the String Quartets of Béla Bartók*](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc2820/), UNT PhD (2001), [PDF](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc2820/m2/1/high_res_d/Dissertation.pdf). Chapters 4–6 provide rhythmic-cell, ostinato and symmetry charts. Concrete targets include Quartet IV/5, mm. 1–89 (Fig. 4.3, p. 58), and Quartet V/5, mm. 17–33 (Figs. 4.30–4.33, pp. 86–87). These are rhythmic analyses, not a fugue-entry database. No open adaptation license verified.

## Bartók: orchestral and choral works

### Music for Strings, Percussion and Celesta, first movement

**Detailed analysis; very high priority.** Richard Adrian Simons, [*Detailed Analysis of the First Movement…*](https://etd.ohiolink.edu/acprod/odb_etd/ws/send_file/send?accession=osu1396370863&disposition=inline), Ohio State M.A. (1962).

Chapter IV, printed pp. 57–66 (PDF 60–69), is unusually granular about stretti, instrumental succession and fragments. The p. 58 chart distinguishes first-stretto statements at 27–30, extension 31–33 and fragments 34–37; later discussion treats inverted material and the closing fragments at 82–85. This is a promising foundation for a full movement map, after independently checking attacks and releases. Some summary and detailed boundaries differ; OCR also corrupts pitch names. Full PDF downloaded and read; no open reuse license or recording alignment established.

Larry Joseph Solomon, [*Symmetry as a Determinant of Musical Composition*](https://researchrepository.wvu.edu/etd/9801/) (1973), offers a complementary graph in Chapter VII, revised 2002: [readable revised chapter](https://www.martijnhooning.nl/analyse/analyse-Schoenberg-Webern-Berg/articles/Webern-Bartok_Symmetry.pdf), Figure 20, PDF p. 3. It is a schematic analysis, not an exhaustive entry table; its idealized pitch cycle has acknowledged exceptions. Durations calculated from score tempi are not recording timings.

### Music for Strings, Percussion and Celesta, returns across movements

**Detailed analysis; high priority for thematic recurrence.** Solomon’s revised chapter above, Figure 24 (PDF p. 9), maps movement III with anchors 1/20/35/45/47/60/73/82 and returning movement-I fragments. Instrument assignments and exact fragment boundaries need transcription. The revised text was read through an educator’s mirror; reuse terms were not established.

[Bartók’s own 1937 structural explanation, reproduced by the Bartók Archive](https://www.zti.hu/bartok/exhibition/en_p8.htm), identifies altered material in II, fragments between III’s arch sections, and diatonic expansion of the first movement’s theme in IV, **203–234**.

László Vikárius, [“Zur Deutung von Dokumenten kompositorischen und analytischen Denkens”](https://www.gmth.de/zeitschrift/artikel/948.aspx), *ZGMTH* 14/2 (2017), 243–261, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.31751/948): section 3, Example 8a–c, compares the opening subject, autograph transformation and **IV:210–215**. This supports cross-movement identity and transformation labels; the whole 203–234 section should not become one presumed subject statement.

**Listening:** [official France Musique performance, Orchestre Philharmonique de Radio France / Alan Gilbert](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HGJcsTtJ188), recorded 16 March 2019, all four movements. Work/performer metadata and embedding availability checked; publisher chapters are not score-to-recording alignment.

### Cantata profana

**Detailed analysis; high priority.** Nathan Lofton, [*A Conductor’s Guide to Béla Bartók’s Cantata Profana*](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/items/6b1da9ca-084c-4f5b-8720-878eb22f40f2), University of Maryland DMA (2024), [PDF](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/bitstreams/022e0c12-ebe0-4b4d-8f28-4d06a21d87a9/download).

Andrew Joseph Anderson’s [2021 Oklahoma dissertation](https://shareok.org/items/ecedbf8b-476e-4797-b674-b421895a5077/full), [PDF](https://shareok.org/bitstreams/63a4a66c-6786-4b05-a1e6-739acfffbef9/download), p. 90, Figure 5.9, provides a useful independent notated entrance comparison. **Detailed analyses; high priority for selected passages.** Cathy Byrne, [“Towards analytical synthesis”](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/9479/), *Maynooth Musicology* 2 (2009), 190–219, [PDF](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/9479/1/Musicology%202%20Byrne.pdf).

Page 210, Table 3, bounds the finale’s development/fugue at **256–383**, with later horn-call/inversion material at 482–555. These are passage/form boundaries, not complete lists of subject statements. Full text inspected; repository article license CC BY-NC-SA 3.0, with underlying scores separate.

Edward Pearsall and John Wm. Schaffer, [“Shape/Interval Contours and Their Ordered Transformations”](https://symposium.music.org/index.php?id=7027&option=com_content&view=article), *College Music Symposium* 45 (2005), Example 10a, examines **I:328–338**: the answer changes the subject’s opening fourth into a fifth while retaining the other relationships. Useful evidence for altered-answer labels that uniform-transposition matching could miss. Publisher text and figure inspected; no annotation export verified.

## Beyond Bartók: especially promising analyses

### Webern, Symphony op. 21

**Detailed analysis; very high priority.** Mark Gotham, [“Analysis Examples—Webern Op. 21 and 24,” Open Music Theory](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/twelve-tone-analysis-examples-webern-op-21-and-24/).

Movement II, Variation 1 (**11–23**), is explained as double canon between violin I/cello and violin II/viola; Variation 7 (**78–88**) has three canonic pairs. The chapter also traces first-movement rows across changes of instrument. This is useful for distinguishing a contrapuntal line from the instrument playing it. Not every row or four-note cell is a theme occurrence. Chapter license **CC BY-SA 4.0**, except separately credited content; no note-addressed annotation export. Check exact score spans before drawing entries.

### Ligeti, Automne à Varsovie

**Detailed analysis; very high priority.** Stephen A. Taylor, [“Chopin, Pygmies, and Tempo Fugue”](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.97.3.3/mto.97.3.3.taylor.html), *MTO* 3/3 (1997), [examples PDF](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.97.3.3/taylor_examples.pdf).

Example 6 reduces **mm. 1–24** into labeled phrases and simultaneous tempo layers; Example 7 treats 55–85; Example 8 supplies a piano-roll representation of 87–98. The article follows the arrival and imitation of additional strands. This is a close conceptual match for Structures, with rows following register/tempo strand rather than piano hand. Exact musical positions must come from the score, not image spacing. Full text and reduction inspected; MTO copyright, no annotation dataset verified.

### Ravel, Le grillon

**Detailed analysis; selected excerpt.** Jessica E. Stankis, [“Maurice Ravel’s ‘Color Counterpoint’ through the Perspective of Japonisme”](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.15.21.1/mto.15.21.1.stankis.html), *MTO* 21/1 (2015).

Table 1 and Examples 1/5 distinguish recurring pianistic gestures and vocal narration in **mm. 1–18**. Paragraph 4.8 locates returns and interruptions, including gesture recurrences at 11 and 16. A useful example of textural characters rather than conventional fugue subjects. Full article/examples inspected; no encoded labels or open adaptation license verified.

### Stravinsky, Requiem Canticles: Exaudi

**Detailed interactive analysis.** Joseph N. Straus, [“Three Stravinsky Analyses”](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.12.18.4/mto.12.18.4.straus.php), *MTO* 18/4 (2012), [Exaudi interface](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.12.18.4/mto_straus_analyses.php?number=3&block=1).

The complete 26-bar movement is presented in five blocks (1–5, 6–11, 12–16, 17–22, 23–26), with motive, texture, serial and symmetry overlays. All five pages and selected explanatory overlays were inspected. It offers a well-bounded thematic-network candidate; aligning labels to choral/instrumental notes still requires transcription. MTO copyright; no downloadable annotation file verified.

### Ligeti, Lux aeterna

**Detailed family-level analysis.** Grant Gershon, “A Conductor’s Guide to Ligeti’s Lux Aeterna,” *International Choral Magazine* XLII/4 (2023), pp. 3–6, [publisher PDF](https://ifcm.net/uploads/icb/2023-10/eicb_2023-4_english.pdf).

The p. 3 table identifies overlapping canon families at **1–37, 39–88, 61–79 and 90–119**; pp. 5–6 give a reduction. This supports a family-level overview. The individual sixteen-part entry/release inventory was not verified, and suggested rehearsal-round entrances must not be mistaken for score data. Full article read; publisher requires permission for reprinting.

### Ruth Crawford Seeger, String Quartet, third movement

**Detailed analysis; a different visual model.** Yi-Cheng Daniel Wu, [“Ruth Crawford’s String Quartet, Mvt. 3: An Analysis of Dynamic Counterpoint, Contour Similarity, and Musical Form”](https://doi.org/10.7202/1066621ar), *Intersections* 37/2 (2017 issue), 123–145, [author-uploaded full text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/338905149_Ruth_Crawford%27s_String_Quartet_Mvt_3_An_Analysis_of_Dynamic_Counterpoint_Contour_Similarity_and_Musical_Form).

Figure 5 marks **63 dynamic contour segments across mm. 1–76**; Table 1 links segments to instrumental dynamic ordering, while later figures map similarity. The full text was inspected through the author’s upload after the publisher presented an access challenge. These labels describe dynamic counterpoint, not recurring melodic subjects: an intensity/contour display would be more faithful than ordinary subject rectangles. No open adaptation license verified.

## Existing analytical data: what can actually be imported?

### CRIM: Renaissance imitation and thematic borrowing

**Encoded human analysis; highest dataset priority.** [CRIM](https://crimproject.org/) publishes attributed contrapuntal observations, MEI scores and relationships between passages. [Observation 11](https://crimproject.org/observations/11/), [JSON](https://crimproject.org/data/observations/11/), records David Fiala’s analysis of Josquin’s *Baisez moy*: Bassus–Tenor canon, ascending fourth, one-breve delay, selected material in mm. 1–38. A linked observation treats related Mass material.

EMA addresses encode selected measures, staves and beats. Preserve their discontinuities and resolve voice names: this example’s `details.voices` numbers differ from MEI staff numbers. Analyses/editions are **CC BY-NC 4.0** under [project terms](https://crimproject.org/about/rights/); source facsimiles are separate. Individual JSON records worked; bulk access was unreliable.

Sylvain Margot and Peter Schubert’s [2023 Baccusi/Rore study](https://crim-essays.crimproject.org/crim-essays-and-explorations/margot-schubert-baccusi/) is another strong starting point. Figure 5 gives motive/voice/measure/beat rows; Figure 6 maps Baccusi’s first Kyrie, mm. 1–22. Figures were inspected, but a downloadable CSV was not verified. Recount occurrences: the prose contains an arithmetic inconsistency.

### BPS-Motif: Beethoven beyond fugues

**Encoded occurrence annotations; reuse terms unresolved.** [Author repository](https://github.com/Wiilly07/Beethoven_motif), [Hsiao et al., ISMIR 2023 paper](https://zenodo.org/records/10265277/files/000032.pdf?download=1).

Contains human-annotated motifs in the **first movements of all 32 piano sonatas**, with note CSVs and occurrence start/end, measure, beat and type. Checked revision: `bd40b774cd6f542076aeb868ca6be83aa5bd61b1`. Staff and MIDI-track numbers are not automatically contrapuntal voice identities; tracks can separate overlapping motifs. Pickup handling and note membership need validation. Synthetic MIDI time is not recorded-performance time.

No explicit dataset license was found in the checked repository. The paper’s CC BY article license does not by itself license the data. Strong research material, pending clarification before wholesale import.

### JKU Patterns Development Database

**Encoded pattern occurrences; reuse terms unresolved.** [Original documentation](https://www.tomcollinsresearch.net/mirex-pattern-discovery-task.html), [preservation mirror](https://github.com/ns2max/jkupdd_dataset).

Five works: Bach BWV 889 fugue; Beethoven Op. 2/1 III; Chopin Op. 24/4; Gibbons’s *The Silver Swan*; Mozart K. 282 II. The checked Gibbons polyphonic reference has four patterns and 26 occurrence CSVs. Occurrence files give onset/pitch pairs; join back to score data for staff and release information, handling ambiguous unisons. Some patterns are cross-voice or sectional, not one melodic line. Monophonic/polyphonic sets differ.

No explicit annotation-data license was verified; the task’s GNU software notice is not a blanket dataset license. Checked mirror revision: `7592734d81c7c9e934ab19af3e1ba12265afa1b9`.

### Multimodal Theme Dataset: useful Bartók seeds

**Supporting theme exemplars with selected alignment, not a recurrence inventory.** [MTD](https://www.audiolabs-erlangen.de/resources/MIR/MTD), [Bartók theme index](https://www.audiolabs-erlangen.de/resources/MIR/MTD-overview/composer/Bartok.html).

The current Bartók index exposes **22 curated theme pages**, with symbolic theme encodings and selected recorded excerpts. For example, [theme 0557](https://www.audiolabs-erlangen.de/resources/MIR/MTD-overview/theme/0557.html) identifies a violin theme from Sz. 112/I and an aligned Chung recording excerpt. This can seed thematic identification, but does not locate all later appearances or orchestral doublings. No blanket reuse grant for all score/audio assets was established; the associated paper’s license is separate.

### Bartók score foundations and other useful corpora

- **[Mikrokosmos-difficulty](https://zenodo.org/records/6092709):** the inspected archive contains 147 MusicXMLs, missing nos. 43, 44, 55, 65, 68 and 127. Metadata provides difficulty labels, not recurring-theme annotations. Repository license CC BY 4.0; OMR-derived notes and edition provenance need checking. Useful alongside the detailed analyses above.
- **[DCML Bartók Bagatelles](https://github.com/DCMLab/bartok_bagatelles):** all 14 pieces, expert harmonic annotations and score/TSV coordinates, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. This is a harmony/score foundation, not an existing thematic inventory.
- **[Dezrann catalog](https://algomus.fr/dezrann/dezrann-catalog.json):** current Mozart sonata resources include form/harmony/cadence labels and selected texture layers. These can supplement theme maps, but do not themselves establish recurring identities. [Format documentation](https://doc.dezrann.net/dez-format); annotation, score and recording terms differ.
- **[Josquin Research Project](https://josquin.stanford.edu/about/):** extensive voice-separated scores and search tools; no comparable exported human recurrence inventory verified here. Use the [current score license](https://github.com/josquin-research-project/jrp-scores/blob/main/LICENSE.txt), CC BY-NC 4.0, and individual notices, rather than older indexed license claims.
- **[Lost Voices](https://digitalduchemin.org/about/):** promising chanson analyses and contrapuntal vocabulary; its About text/footer give conflicting reuse terms. A follow-up research lead.
- **[Polyrhythm Project](https://polyrhythm.humdrum.org/overview/):** many relevant twentieth-century score excerpts, but perusal restrictions prevent treating it as an open import corpus. Excerpts are not theme-occurrence annotations.

## Supporting Bartók resources and acquisition leads

- **David Yeomans, [*Bartók for Piano: A Survey of His Solo Literature*](https://publish.iupress.indiana.edu/projects/bartok-for-piano)** (1988; paperback reissue 2000): a broad repertoire and pedagogical survey, including the Four Dialogues and all *Mikrokosmos* volumes. Useful for choosing further pieces, but not a verified complete entry inventory. The project page identifies CC BY-NC-ND, while the reader footer displays a different license; clarify before adapting substantial material. The project description and indexed excerpts were inspected; the reader body was not fully retrieved.
- **[Bartók Complete Critical Edition: related publications](https://bartok-osszkiadas.zti.hu/en/related-publications/)**: a strong bibliography for recent specialist work and links to Nakahara’s dissertation. Use it to follow up critical-edition research; it is not an analysis dataset.
- **László Vikárius, *Cantata profana: Bartók’s Sacred Bridge* (2025), [chapter 5](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780190083236.003.0006), pp. 161–214:** a particularly relevant acquisition lead. Publisher abstract inspected; restricted chapter contents were not verified.
- **Marcia Beach, [*Bartok’s Fifth String Quartet: Studies in Genesis and Structure*](https://urresearch.rochester.edu/institutionalPublicationPublicView.action?institutionalItemId=36290):** repository confirms text, musical-example and facsimile volumes. Scans were obtained but not read sufficiently to establish passage-level claims.
- **Daphne Leong, [“Metric Conflict in the First Movement of Bartók’s Sonata for Two Pianos and Percussion”](https://www.colorado.edu/music/sites/default/files/attached-files/leong_metric_conflict_in_bartok_s2pp.pdf), *Theory and Practice* 24 (1999), 57–90:** author-hosted scan obtained; detailed examples remain to be audited.

## From a source to a diagram

For each selected passage, preserve analyst and page/figure provenance, exact edition and measure numbering, identified parts, thematic identity, variant and coverage. Check both onset and final written release. A formal section, rhythmic cell, dynamic contour and complete subject are different objects and should remain visibly distinct.

My recommended first research-to-diagram batch is **Duo 37, Mikrokosmos 141, Quartet 5/I and MSPC I/III**, followed by **Webern op. 21/II and Ligeti’s Automne à Varsovie**. CRIM is the strongest parallel route for importing pre-existing analysis at scale. These are proposals; this research update adds no new viewer entries or timing maps.

Listening links above are candidates tied to named works/performers. Existing pieces awaiting manual synchronization remain in the separate [manual timing checklist](manual-timing.md). Future research candidates should enter that checklist only after their score diagrams and specific recordings have been selected.
